# 01 · Dramaturgy and screenwriting

Start with the viewer's experience of an original idea. Define a controlling action or question, a motive, a cost, and an emotional effect. Write beats and then test their legibility, causal links and duration. Use image, behavior, sound and speech as different ways of carrying information.

The rulebook uses **priority 1** for common decisions that often affect the whole short, **priority 2** for situational tools, and **priority 3** for specialized techniques. Confidence describes the support for the stated scope. A highly supported technique still has exceptions.

## Rules

### DRA-001

**Unify the action** · dramaturgical principle · priority 1 · high confidence

Organize the film around one consequential action, relationship change or inquiry.

**Why it works.** The viewer can infer how each beat bears on a recognizable whole.

**Apply.** Narrative shorts and first-pass beat lists. **Exception.** An episodic or associative film can unify through a question, motif or a transformation in the viewer's understanding.

**Example.** The search for a misplaced object also tests whether its owner will accept help. **Common mistake.** Including every event that happened to the protagonist that day.

**LLM action.** Write one controlling action or organizing experience. Tag the contribution of each scene to it. **Check.** Remove one scene on paper. Keep it if a later cause, emotional interpretation or significant pattern changes.

**Origin.** Aristotle's account of plot unity, applied to the short form. [SRC-ARIS-POE](../sources/bibliography.md#src-aris-poe) · [SRC-NYU-GRAD](../sources/bibliography.md#src-nyu-grad)

**Related.** `DRA-006`, `DRA-015`

### DRA-002

**Link decisions and consequences** · dramaturgical principle · priority 1 · high confidence

Give each major beat a cause and a consequence. Use plausibility within the film's established world.

**Why it works.** An audience can connect decisions, obstacles and results across an ellipsis.

**Apply.** Plot-driven stories and moments that change the available options. **Exception.** Accident can initiate a story, or express the intended instability of its world. Make clear what it changes for the character.

**Example.** In The Black Hole the device enables each increasingly risky choice and the final trap follows from that use. **Common mistake.** Rescuing a blocked character with an ability introduced at the moment it is needed.

**LLM action.** Store a cause, action and visible effect for each turn. Flag leaps as deliberate ellipses, mysteries or gaps. **Check.** A reader can connect the resolution to an earlier condition or choice without inventing a new event.

**Origin.** Aristotle on probable or necessary sequence, with a short-film application. [SRC-ARIS-POE](../sources/bibliography.md#src-aris-poe) · [SRC-FILTA-BLACK](../sources/bibliography.md#src-filta-black)

**Related.** `DRA-001`, `DRA-007`, `DRA-009`

### DRA-003

**Separate goal, motive, and need** · dramaturgical principle · priority 1 · high confidence

Give the character a concrete pursuit, a reason to pursue it and, if the premise calls for one, a deeper need.

**Why it works.** The goal organizes action. The motive gives it weight. A gap between goal and need can create an arc.

**Apply.** Character-led narratives and revision of passive scenes. **Exception.** An observational short may follow a recurring behavior or attention shift. A stable character can test another person's assumptions.

**Example.** A brother wants a signed form today to avoid losing a place. His refusal to ask for help may reveal a separate need. **Common mistake.** Using one abstract label such as freedom for every field.

**LLM action.** Write goal as a verb with a target and success condition. State motive and optional need separately. Attach observable evidence to each. **Check.** Someone can identify success or failure from a planned action and explain why it matters to this person.

**Origin.** Truby's distinction between desire and need, with Egri's emphasis on behavior. [SRC-TRUB-TALK](../sources/bibliography.md#src-trub-talk) · [SRC-EGRI-ART](../sources/bibliography.md#src-egri-art) · [SRC-PIX-PIPER](../sources/bibliography.md#src-pix-piper)

**Related.** `DRA-004`, `DRA-005`, `DRA-016`

### DRA-004

**Escalate cost or resistance** · dramaturgical principle · priority 1 · high confidence

Make the pursuit meet resistance and change the cost, options, information or tactic as the story proceeds.

**Why it works.** The audience can distinguish a meaningful escalation from repeated action.

**Apply.** Goal-led scenes, comedy, conflict and suspense. **Exception.** Repetition can build ritual, dread or comedy when a meaningful detail changes or the repetition itself becomes the subject.

**Example.** The waves in Piper make the food-seeking goal difficult through a readable feature of the location. **Common mistake.** Adding more obstacles of the same kind while the character behaves the same way.

**LLM action.** Give each attempt an obstacle and a changed state. Track the consequence of failure in concrete terms. **Check.** Explain how the second attempt puts a different option or cost in play.

**Origin.** Egri on conflict arising from character and Truby on opponents. [SRC-EGRI-ART](../sources/bibliography.md#src-egri-art) · [SRC-TRUB-TALK](../sources/bibliography.md#src-trub-talk) · [SRC-PIX-PIPER](../sources/bibliography.md#src-pix-piper)

**Related.** `DRA-002`, `DRA-003`, `DRA-006`

### DRA-005

**Turn the scene** · dramaturgical principle · priority 1 · high confidence

Give a scene an objective and a discernible change in knowledge, leverage, relation, options or feeling.

**Why it works.** The audience gets a reason to remember the scene and a different situation for the next beat.

**Apply.** Dramatic scene breakdown and edits that feel static. **Exception.** A deliberate hold may establish a baseline, place or rhythm. Name what the duration or accumulation contributes.

**Example.** A request begins as a favor and becomes a test of loyalty when the cost is disclosed. **Common mistake.** A busy scene repeats information the audience already knows.

**LLM action.** Record entry state, immediate objective, pivot and exit state. Mark whether a character or only the audience registers it. **Check.** Read the entry and exit states aloud. If the distinction is hard to name, check the scene's purpose or combine it.

**Origin.** McKee's scene-value exercise, broadened for observational and tonal work. [SRC-MCK-TURN](../sources/bibliography.md#src-mck-turn) · [SRC-AFI-DIR](../sources/bibliography.md#src-afi-dir)

**Related.** `DRA-003`, `DRA-006`, `DRA-015`

### DRA-006

**Track one dramatic question** · creative heuristic · priority 2 · medium confidence

Give the viewer something specific to wonder about and update the answer as circumstances change.

**Why it works.** A question can carry interest through quiet passages, action or limited exposition.

**Apply.** Loglines, openings and narrative scene order. **Exception.** An essay film can orient attention with a tension or motif that invites reflection without a yes-or-no answer.

**Example.** Will the seller accept the exchange after she recognizes the buyer? **Common mistake.** Asking vague rhetorical questions without a way to infer an answer.

**LLM action.** Store a main question plus the beat that raises it, later changes it and resolves or deliberately leaves it open. **Check.** A fresh reader can state what the film made them anticipate and what the ending did with it.

**Origin.** Landau's discussion of anticipation, adapted to short-film planning. [SRC-LAND-TALK](../sources/bibliography.md#src-land-talk) · [SRC-BORD-BOMB](../sources/bibliography.md#src-bord-bomb)

**Related.** `DRA-001`, `DRA-005`, `DRA-010`, `DRA-017`

### DRA-007

**Earn the character arc** · dramaturgical principle · priority 2 · medium confidence

Let a significant choice reveal change, failure to change, or a principle tested under new pressure.

**Why it works.** An observable decision under pressure gives the viewer evidence of a changed relationship, belief or priority.

**Apply.** Stories promising transformation or revelation. **Exception.** A stable protagonist can change the setting, another person, or the audience's interpretation. State whose state shifts.

**Example.** A reluctant collaborator volunteers the last remaining resource after earlier guarding it. **Common mistake.** Announcing growth in final dialogue while the behavior remains ambiguous for accidental reasons.

**LLM action.** Pair an early choice with a later choice under comparable or greater cost. State the before and after inference. **Check.** A viewer can point to behavior that supports the claimed change or resolve.

**Origin.** Truby on weakness and need and Aristotle on recognition, with an optional stable arc. [SRC-TRUB-TALK](../sources/bibliography.md#src-trub-talk) · [SRC-ARIS-POE](../sources/bibliography.md#src-aris-poe)

**Related.** `DRA-002`, `DRA-003`, `DRA-005`, `DRA-017`

### DRA-008

**Pay off what the film emphasizes** · dramaturgical principle · priority 1 · high confidence

If framing, repetition, sound or dialogue presents a detail as consequential, plan how it will matter or how the expectation will be deliberately redirected.

**Why it works.** Audiences track prominent details and use their return to infer coherence or reinterpretation.

**Apply.** Distinctive props, stated abilities, rules of the world and explicit promises. **Exception.** A detail can pay off through atmosphere, thematic echo, character or an intentional redirection. Background texture can simply establish the world.

**Example.** A latch shown to stick returns when a character needs to leave quietly. **Common mistake.** Giving a decorative prop an emphatic insert and forgetting the implication.

**LLM action.** Keep a ledger of setup beat, attention cue, expected meaning, later function and carry-over prop state. **Check.** Every strongly emphasized setup has a later function or an explicit reason to stay open.

**Origin.** Anton Chekhov's advice on a monologue that creates a promise, applied to screen emphasis. [SRC-CHEK-GUN](../sources/bibliography.md#src-chek-gun) · [SRC-CHEK-CHR](../sources/bibliography.md#src-chek-chr) · [SRC-CHEK-PLAY](../sources/bibliography.md#src-chek-play)

**Related.** `DRA-002`, `DRA-009`, `DRA-015`

### DRA-009

**Plant a fair reversal** · dramaturgical principle · priority 2 · high confidence

Prepare a turn with evidence the audience can reinterpret once it has the missing information.

**Why it works.** A turn can be unexpected on first viewing and legible on reflection.

**Apply.** Reveals, mysteries, comic reversals and discoveries. **Exception.** Absurdism can use an unprepared break as the intended event. Clarify that the effect is rupture.

**Example.** A repeated look at an exit initially reads as fear, then gains a different meaning when the character uses it to help someone. **Common mistake.** Withholding a basic fact that all shots would ordinarily reveal, then calling the reveal a twist.

**LLM action.** Track cue, first plausible reading, later reading and action that changes because of the reveal. **Check.** A fresh viewer can identify a real earlier cue after the reveal and explain its changed meaning.

**Origin.** Aristotle on reversal and recognition, with Chekhov's treatment of expectation. [SRC-ARIS-POE](../sources/bibliography.md#src-aris-poe) · [SRC-CHEK-GUN](../sources/bibliography.md#src-chek-gun)

**Related.** `DRA-002`, `DRA-008`, `DRA-010`

### DRA-010

**Control what the viewer knows** · established technique · priority 2 · high confidence

Choose which story facts the viewer and each key character have at each beat. Decide how long to sustain any gap.

**Why it works.** An information gap can create dramatic irony or anticipatory suspense. Shared uncertainty can create tension from a character's immediate experience.

**Apply.** Suspense, surprise, deception, mystery and relationship scenes. **Exception.** An omniscient narrator or chorus can make information shared and keep interest through consequence, rhythm or tone.

**Example.** A viewer sees a character discard the only key before the other person confidently promises to open the door. **Common mistake.** Revealing danger after the scene and expecting tension before the reveal.

**LLM action.** Record viewer knowledge, focal character knowledge, source shot or line, and the intended effect of showing or withholding the fact. **Check.** For each reveal, specify who first knows it and what changes if it is moved earlier.

**Origin.** Hitchcock's illustration and Bordwell's analysis of narration, extended to a beat ledger. [SRC-BORD-BOMB](../sources/bibliography.md#src-bord-bomb) · [SRC-LAND-TALK](../sources/bibliography.md#src-land-talk)

**Related.** `DRA-006`, `DRA-009`, `DRA-012`

### DRA-011

**Put exposition inside action** · established technique · priority 1 · high confidence

Give essential information through behavior, an object being used, environmental reaction, a sound cue or a line spoken to achieve something.

**Why it works.** The viewer learns the world while something consequential is happening.

**Apply.** Openings, visual storytelling and exposition-heavy scenes. **Exception.** Speech, voice-over or a title may be the right dramatic form when the act of telling is meaningful or the fact needs clarity.

**Example.** A character calmly replaces the same worn fuse by touch, showing a practiced relationship to the place. **Common mistake.** Stopping the story for a line that explains the action already on screen.

**LLM action.** Assign each necessary fact a carrier, recipient, and moment. Use a low-cost still or sound when a new generated shot adds little. **Check.** Mute or remove the explanatory line. Check whether essential information survives or restore the part that does useful work.

**Origin.** Hitchcock's AFI master class and NYU's visual exercise. [SRC-HITCH-AFI](../sources/bibliography.md#src-hitch-afi) · [SRC-NYU-GRAD](../sources/bibliography.md#src-nyu-grad)

**Related.** `DRA-005`, `DRA-012`, `DRA-013`, `DRA-015`

### DRA-012

**Write playable subtext** · established technique · priority 2 · medium confidence

Give the character a reason to avoid saying something directly. Let the line and the action reveal enough for an inference.

**Why it works.** The viewer can feel the pressure between the surface tactic and the underlying aim.

**Apply.** Scenes of negotiation, intimacy, concealment or social restraint. **Exception.** A direct declaration can work when honesty is difficult and saying it changes the situation.

**Example.** In Act IV of The Cherry Orchard, the talk of travel and ordinary details carries the failure to say something consequential. **Common mistake.** Withholding all observable clues and expecting an abstract emotion to read.

**LLM action.** Record literal line, playable intent, barrier and visible cue. Keep the hidden aim in direction notes and express the cue in the performance prompt. **Check.** A reader can propose the intended subtext and cite a line, gesture or pause that supports it.

**Origin.** A close reading of Anton Chekhov's play, combined with actor-oriented scene work. [SRC-CHEK-PLAY](../sources/bibliography.md#src-chek-play) · [SRC-AFI-DIR](../sources/bibliography.md#src-afi-dir)

**Related.** `DRA-003`, `DRA-005`, `DRA-010`, `DRA-013`, `DRA-014`

### DRA-013

**Give dialogue a dramatic job** · creative heuristic · priority 2 · medium confidence

Keep dialogue that pursues, conceals, changes, specifies or gives a character an unmistakable voice.

**Why it works.** Purposeful lines develop a relationship or carry information the rest of the scene cannot convey as precisely.

**Apply.** Script and timing passes, especially in dialogue-heavy shorts. **Exception.** Repetition, banter or a monologue can serve performance, comic rhythm, social pressure or the act of telling.

**Example.** One character repeats a polite invitation after being refused, turning the phrase into pressure. **Common mistake.** Adding a line that paraphrases a clear action or tells the other character facts they both know.

**LLM action.** Tag the work of each line. Read it aloud at the intended performance pace and move essential information to a clearer carrier if needed. **Check.** Try removing or shortening the line. Compare voice, intent, legibility and timing before deciding.

**Origin.** Hitchcock on action-bearing exposition and the NYU sync-sound exercise, adapted to script revision. [SRC-HITCH-AFI](../sources/bibliography.md#src-hitch-afi) · [SRC-NYU-GRAD](../sources/bibliography.md#src-nyu-grad) · [SRC-CHEK-PLAY](../sources/bibliography.md#src-chek-play)

**Related.** `DRA-011`, `DRA-012`, `DRA-014`, `DRA-015`

### DRA-014

**Write silence as an active beat** · established technique · priority 2 · medium confidence

Specify what happens in a pause, what the audience can hear and what ends the silence.

**Why it works.** A pause creates room to process a change, hear an environment or experience a character's decision.

**Apply.** After a refusal, before a choice, or when an action and a sound cue carry a transition. **Exception.** A deliberately numb or abstract interval may have little visible movement. Use duration and sound consciously.

**Example.** After an unanswered request, a hand holds on the door frame while footsteps recede. **Common mistake.** Inserting a pause as a fixed number of seconds without a performance or sound purpose.

**LLM action.** Specify silence type, observable action, room tone or off-screen cue and the event that ends the hold. **Check.** The hold changes attention or meaning in a timed rehearsal. Compare a shorter version if it stalls.

**Origin.** The silence and recurring off-screen sound in The Cherry Orchard, applied to film. [SRC-CHEK-PLAY](../sources/bibliography.md#src-chek-play) · [SRC-NYU-GRAD](../sources/bibliography.md#src-nyu-grad)

**Related.** `DRA-005`, `DRA-012`, `DRA-013`, `DRA-017`

### DRA-015

**Compress between consequential beats** · creative heuristic · priority 1 · high confidence

Spend runtime on actions that change the story or how the audience experiences it. Let viewers infer routine connective action when the link is clear.

**Why it works.** Selective omission gives meaningful action and reaction enough screen time.

**Apply.** Runtime pressure, travel, repeated explanations and low-value coverage. **Exception.** A journey, ritual or wait can be the emotional center. Preserve enough duration to feel its effect.

**Example.** Cut from a refusal to a changed arrival when place and elapsed time are apparent. **Common mistake.** Removing the reaction that makes a fast plot beat emotionally intelligible.

**LLM action.** Tag beats by dramatic, sensory or orienting function. Merge duplicate functions and allocate seconds to meaningful action and recovery. **Check.** A timed read or animatic fits with space to understand the critical turn. Test every omitted link for ambiguity.

**Origin.** Aristotle's unity test and NYU's limited-scope projects, adapted to short production. [SRC-ARIS-POE](../sources/bibliography.md#src-aris-poe) · [SRC-NYU-GRAD](../sources/bibliography.md#src-nyu-grad) · [SRC-MCK-TURN](../sources/bibliography.md#src-mck-turn)

**Related.** `DRA-001`, `DRA-005`, `DRA-011`, `DRA-016`

### DRA-016

**Choose structure to fit the intention** · creative heuristic · priority 2 · high confidence

Use a structure when it clarifies the intended action or emotion. Compare one alternative if the first structure adds obligations the premise does not need.

**Why it works.** Different forms organize attention through consequence, repetition, discovery, encounter or contrast.

**Apply.** Outline development and stalled revisions. **Exception.** A requested form or genre constraint can set the pattern. Keep the parts that make that pattern legible.

**Example.** A repeated encounter is organized around changes in behavior. It can use a loop with variation even if it has no quest. **Common mistake.** Scaling a feature-film page formula to seconds and creating scenes solely to meet it.

**LLM action.** Choose a structural lens and record the jobs of the beats. Keep one rejected alternative and why it fit less well. **Check.** Read the beats without their structural labels. The viewer should still follow the planned experience.

**Origin.** Field, Campbell, Vogler, Landau and rulebook synthesis for shorts. [SRC-FIELD-CASE](../sources/bibliography.md#src-field-case) · [SRC-CAMP-HERO](../sources/bibliography.md#src-camp-hero) · [SRC-VOGL-TALK](../sources/bibliography.md#src-vogl-talk) · [SRC-LAND-TALK](../sources/bibliography.md#src-land-talk)

**Related.** `DRA-001`, `DRA-006`, `DRA-007`, `DRA-015`

### DRA-017

**Land the consequence and the afterbeat** · dramaturgical principle · priority 1 · high confidence

Show the outcome of the governing action, or make the intended uncertainty perceptible. Give the viewer time to experience the consequence.

**Why it works.** An ending can resolve action while leaving an emotional, ethical or thematic question active.

**Apply.** Climax and final scene design. **Exception.** An abrupt cut can make unresolved tension the intended last sensation. Establish what remains open before the cut.

**Example.** A door closes after the decision. A short off-screen sound changes how the audience reads the empty room. **Common mistake.** Adding a speech that repeats the conclusion after the audience has already understood the consequence.

**LLM action.** Record final choice, consequence, last image or sound, and the question that remains open if any. Allocate a short edit handle separately from the story runtime. **Check.** After a read or animatic, ask what happened, what is still uncertain, and what the last beat made the viewer feel.

**Origin.** Aristotle on completeness and a close reading of the final sound cues in The Cherry Orchard. [SRC-ARIS-POE](../sources/bibliography.md#src-aris-poe) · [SRC-CHEK-PLAY](../sources/bibliography.md#src-chek-play) · [SRC-LAND-TALK](../sources/bibliography.md#src-land-talk)

**Related.** `DRA-002`, `DRA-006`, `DRA-007`, `DRA-008`, `DRA-014`

## Structure selection

| Structure | Useful when | Test at short length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Setup, confrontation, resolution | The viewer needs to track a pursuit and its consequence. Field's paradigm supplies useful functions. | Can one beat establish the situation and the goal? Remove feature-length page targets. [SRC-FIELD-CASE](../sources/bibliography.md#src-field-case) |
| Character choice and consequence | A premise is tested through behavior. Egri and Truby help distinguish motive, desire, resistance and need. | Show two choices under different pressure. | Compressed mythic journey | Crossing a boundary, facing a trial or returning altered gives the premise an intelligible form. | Use only the functions with dramatic work to do. Campbell's comparative framework and Vogler's screenplay adaptation have different scopes. [SRC-CAMP-HERO](../sources/bibliography.md#src-camp-hero) · [SRC-VOGL-TALK](../sources/bibliography.md#src-vogl-talk) |
| Single encounter, loop with variation, or associative progression | The intended change is a shift in a relationship, a motif, or a viewer's perception. | Identify a change in information, behavior, sound or image that lets the viewer feel progression. McKee gives a scene-turn diagnostic. An observational or durational beat can earn its time by changing the viewer's attention or expectations. Truby describes external desire and internal need, and a stable protagonist can give the viewer a changing interpretation. Landau and Vogler both describe flexible use of structural patterns. Use the test that fits the promise of the short. ## Handoff to directing

For every beat record its ID, character, playable objective, resistance, action, visible or audible result, audience knowledge, entry and exit state, and estimated edit duration. Record the setup and payoff beat IDs and the dramatic intention for the last image or sound. Pass intent and constraints to the shot-planning stage. Keep runtime estimates separate from generation clip limits.

Review in three passes. First read for cause and effect. Next mark what the viewer knows and what changes emotionally. Finally time a read or animatic and remove duplicate dramatic work while protecting necessary reactions, orientation, and silence.
